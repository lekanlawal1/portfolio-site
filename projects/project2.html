AI Support Ticket Triage: LLM Pipeline with Guardrails

An LLM sorts incoming support tickets by category, priority, and routing, engineered around the assumption that the AI will sometimes be wrong.

PythonGemini APIstructured output Streamlitprompt engineering

The story

Support desks burn 3–8 minutes of skilled attention per ticket just deciding what it is and who should handle it. This tool automates that first pass with Gemini, but the interesting part isn't the API call, it's everything wrapped around it. The model is forced into a JSON schema (no free-text parsing), reports a confidence score, and below 75% confidence a ticket is never auto-routed: it goes to a human review queue with the model's guess attached as a suggestion. Rate limits, timeouts, and malformed responses get exponential-backoff retries; if everything fails, the ticket lands in the review queue with the reason logged. The batch never crashes and never silently drops a ticket.

Evaluated on 171 labeled synthetic tickets, including 15 deliberately ambiguous ones, the evaluation caught something better than a good score: a systematic prompt bug. Complaints that mention money were misfiled as billing at 95% confidence, traceable to one tie-breaking rule in my prompt. The lesson, documented in the repo: confidence gates catch vagueness, not bias, and shipping an LLM feature means shipping its monitoring.

Key decisions I'd defend in an interview

  • Confidence gate over maximizing accuracy: the goal isn't zero errors; it's that errors get caught. A low-confidence mistake costs 4 minutes of human review; only high-confidence errors are true failures, and they're measured.
  • Two documented prompt iterations: v1's failures (invented categories, priority inflation for ALL-CAPS customers) each map to a specific v2 fix.
  • Free-tier rate limits treated as a design constraint: throttling, backoff, and resumable batches, not an afterthought.
